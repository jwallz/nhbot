Two tax rates, and why they differ

Every New Hampshire town publishes a property-tax rate — dollars per $1,000 of value. But there are really two rates, and confusing them is the single most common mistake in comparing towns.

The advertised rate — what you pay

This is the rate on your tax bill. The town multiplies it by its own assessed value of your property. It is the number people know and the one this site shows first. But it is not comparable between towns, because towns assess at different fractions of true market value.

The equalized rate — for comparing towns

Assessments drift below market value between revaluations (NH requires a full revaluation only every five years). Each year the Department of Revenue Administration runs a ratio study — comparing recent sale prices to assessed values — to find each town's equalization ratio, the percentage of market value at which it assesses. Divide the town's assessed value by that ratio and you get its full-market (equalized) valuation; the equalized tax rate is the rate the town would charge on that full value.

equalized rate ≈ advertised rate × equalization ratio

A worked example

Say a town assesses at 67% of market value and its advertised rate is $23.98. A home worth $745,000 is assessed around $500,000, so the bill is 500 × 23.98 ≈ $11,990. Express that same bill against the home's full $745,000 market value and the rate is about $16 — the equalized rate. Nobody pays $16; it is the advertised rate translated onto a full-market base so it can be compared with any other town.

Why the equalized rate can look "too low"

Whenever a town assesses below market (ratio under 100%), its equalized rate is lower than its advertised rate — sometimes dramatically. That is not an error and not a discount; it is the same tax burden expressed on a larger base.

Revaluation re-bases the rate — it is not a tax cut

When a town completes a revaluation and assessments jump to full market value, the advertised rate falls toward the old equalized rate. Taxes, in aggregate, don't change — the town still raises the same money; it is now spread over a larger assessed base. Your individual bill shifts only if your property gained more or less value than the town average.

Which should you use?

To understand your own bill, use the advertised rate. To ask "is this town more expensive than that one?", use the equalized rate — it is the only apples-to-apples comparison. This site shows both, everywhere.

Source: NH Department of Revenue Administration, Equalization Bureau.
